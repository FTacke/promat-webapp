Capturing the Pronunciation of Learners of German: Data Collection Design for the German Corpus of *Pronunciation Matters*

<!-- p3 -->
This article presents the data collection design of the German learner corpus of *Pronunciation Matters*. The aim of the corpus is to make frequent pronunciation difficulties of learners of German with different linguistic backgrounds easier to experience and more accessible for research and teaching. In what follows, the design of the elicitation materials – a wordlist and a sentence list as well as a guided interview – is explained in terms of its phonetic-phonological motivation and its relevance for the project and for the teaching of German as a foreign and second language.

<!-- p5 -->
Objectives

<!-- p6 -->
Acquiring the “correct” pronunciation of a foreign language is a topic that is omnipresent in foreign language teaching – after all, it is directly linked to all other properties of a language –, but which is nevertheless often neglected in favour of other skills, for various reasons. Research on pronunciation in German as a foreign and second language has grown considerably since the 1990s; nevertheless, there is a lack of concrete databases and corpora that systematically document learners’ pronunciation and make it accessible. *Pronunciation Matters* therefore aims to help close this gap by providing a platform with pronunciation data collected under controlled conditions, intended to support pronunciation research and the professional development of teachers.

<!-- p7 -->
By means of a standardised wordlist and sentence list, the focus is not only placed on the problems that result from a particular L1; the pronunciation problems of learners with different first languages can also be compared.

<!-- p9 -->
Selection of the phenomena: (A)typical pronunciation difficulties of learners of German

<!-- p10 -->
Since the German corpus was not meant to cover learners with one particular L1 only, the design of the corpus centred on the question of how the pronunciation of learners with different L1s can be captured systematically and made accessible for different research questions. So that learners of all linguistic backgrounds as well as possibly unexpected problems can be captured, the corpus therefore deliberately takes into account a broader range of phonetic-phonological phenomena that are salient in German. The orientation here was the standardised pronunciation of German as described, for example, by Krech et al. (2009).

<!-- p11 -->
Since the German vowel system, with 16 phonemes, is very complex (the average size of a vowel inventory in a worldwide comparison is 4–5 phonemes (cf. Maddieson 2013)), it represents a major hurdle for learners of German. For this reason, all 16 vowel phonemes of German were integrated into the design of the materials. Depending on the L1, different aspects can prove difficult, e.g. the umlauts tend to be harder to acquire for speakers of Italian than for French-speaking learners, who also have these phonemes in their sound inventory (cf. Dahmen et al. 2021). Many learners, for instance, have problems with the distinction of vowel length, which in German always correlates with particular vowel phonemes (quality) (cf. Krech et al. 2009: 25). The short vowels are generally articulated slightly less tense, which is not always easy for learners to hear. For this reason, care was taken for all vowels that they each occur with the correlating features [+long] and/or [+short], so that possible problems in distinguishing vowel quantity can also be brought out. In addition, the standard pronunciation has three diphthongs: [aɛ̯], [aɔ̯] and [ɔœ̯] (cf. Krech et al., 2009, p. 26), which were likewise included in the design of the materials.

For the consonants, too, the aim was to integrate as broad a range of different phonemes and phoneme combinations as possible. Among other things, <r> was used in its consonantal and its vocalic form, as were other sounds that frequently cause learners problems, e.g. both <ch> allophones and the glottal stop [ʔ]. Finally, frequent phonological processes such as final obstruent devoicing and schwa elision were also included, since they are integral parts of German pronunciation.

<!-- p19 -->
Design of the materials

<!-- p20 -->
To elicit the phenomena, a standardised wordlist and sentence list were chosen, on the one hand to ensure comparability among the speakers themselves and on the other hand to be able to record all required phenomena both in isolation and in context.

<!-- p21 -->
The wordlist of the German corpus comprises a total of 96 items, 85 of them individual words and 11 minimal pairs. The minimal pairs are intended to cover frequent errors that arise, among other things, from difficulties in discriminating or producing two similar sounds (e.g. [o] vs. [ɔ] or [ç] vs. [ʃ]). For this reason, the selection of the words also took into account the grapheme-phoneme correspondence, which in German plays a major role, e.g. for vowel length. Wherever possible, several spellings of a phoneme were therefore always used, e.g. <f> and <v> for [f]. Since Munro (2021), among others, found that correct pronunciation often goes hand in hand with learning a word, preference was also given predominantly to high-frequency words that are not too complex and can also be read by learners at lower proficiency levels. All selected phenomena are represented several times, by at least 2–3 items, in order to ensure the reliability of the results, since the production of a sound can also vary from word to word (cf. Munro 2022). Some phonemes, such as [t] or [n], occur more frequently in the wordlist, however, since owing to their high frequency they occur more often in words that have a different target phoneme. In addition, attention was paid to the distribution of the individual phenomena within the words, so that vowels and consonants occur in different items partly word-initially and partly word-medially / -finally, in order to be able to draw conclusions about the influence of position on pronunciation. For quality assurance, the final selection of items was reviewed by the phonetics expert Dr. Gea de Jong-Lendle (https://www.uni-marburg.de/en/fb09/institutes/german-linguistics/phonetics/team/dejong).

<!-- p22 -->
In order to examine whether embedding in a syntactic context influences the pronunciation of the items from the wordlist, a second elicitation instrument was also necessary. A reading text or a sentence list were considered for this purpose. An authentic reading text carried the risk both of introducing too many new items and, at the same time, of not covering all desired phenomena. At the same time, a long, coherent text can potentially be too demanding lexically and syntactically for learners at lower proficiency levels, which could also affect pronunciation if concentration is directed too much towards the content (cf. Tacke 2026). A text written with the items from the wordlist was also conceivable; however, it would have been difficult to spin a semantically coherent story from them, which in turn could also be distracting. So as not to overburden the speakers and still be able to elicit all phenomena, a sentence list was therefore chosen. For this purpose, the items were recombined in sentence contexts, so that each sentence contains approximately two items from the wordlist. These items are taken over in lexically identical form. Here, too, care was taken not to make the sentences too long or too complex and to avoid semantic oddities as far as possible. The list comprises 50 sentences: 31 declarative sentences, 10 yes/no questions and 10 wh-questions. The questions were included in order to gain possible insights into prosody as well, without having to introduce a further elicitation instrument.

<!-- p23 -->
Finally, a guided interview was conducted to find out how the learners experienced the reading process and how they perceived their errors. This self-assessment can help to gain insight into the possible origins of the respective pronunciation problems.

<!-- p25 -->
Conclusion

<!-- p26 -->
Through its systematic design, the German corpus of *Pronunciation Matters* offers good insight into the authentic pronunciation of learners of German and thereby opens up a wide range of possibilities for empirical research on L2 pronunciation as well as for learner and teacher education. Owing to its broad design, the model can be applied to learners with the most diverse linguistic backgrounds and can thus be used in many different ways. Of course, it must be borne in mind that the scope is limited by the length of the wordlist, so that, although a very broad spectrum of phonemes was used, not all combinations that can influence pronunciation could be included.

<!-- p27 -->
Nevertheless – above all through its multi-step design – the corpus represents a versatile and practical tool for research and teaching and can thus make a valuable contribution to pronunciation training in foreign language teaching.
